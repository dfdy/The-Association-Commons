## Purpose {#purpose}

This white paper develops the conceptual, legal and institutional basis of the Democratic Development Commons (DDC): a South African framework through which people can form voluntary associations, govern themselves democratically, learn through collective action, practise mutual aid, steward commons and federate without surrendering local self-government. It treats disability access, substantive equality and the legal conditions of association as integral to democratic design rather than as later compliance additions.

A central practical aim is to develop a **configurable constitution-generating instrument** through which different forms of South African association can turn these commitments into constitutions they understand, adopt and revise themselves. A key rationale is the fragmented landscape of South African laws, regulatory requirements and associational customs: groups need a way to bring these together into a coherent, usable constitution while distinguishing binding legal duties from practices members can choose, adapt or contest. The instrument should make constitution-making a process of collective learning and democratic choice. It must help associations harness enabling features of current law, recognise activity-specific duties, compensate for legal gaps, and translate democratic commitments into usable rules without imposing one organisational model.

Part I develops the framework; Part II offers a provisional public distillation for the website; and Part III examines critical challenges and future considerations to guide research, participatory testing and constitutional design. Together they provide the conceptual basis and design requirements for this future constitution generator while keeping its assumptions open to scrutiny and revision.

## Executive Summary {#executive-summary}

The Democratic Development Commons (DDC) is a South African framework for voluntary association, collective learning, mutual aid, commons governance and federated self-government. Its central proposition is simple: **people develop democratic capacity by exercising it together**. Development is therefore not something delivered to passive beneficiaries, nor is it reducible to economic growth, employability, professionalisation or marketable skills. It concerns the open-ended transformation of the conditions through which people can understand their circumstances, govern shared institutions, care for one another, create knowledge, contest domination and pursue human and ecological flourishing.

The framework responds to a specifically South African history. People's Education, Workers' Education, trade unions, civics, burial societies, savings clubs, stokvels, churches and faith communities, mutual-aid societies, political education groups, student organisations and neighbourhood formations have all generated practical knowledge about democratic association. Vally's account is especially important: anti-apartheid popular and worker education treated struggle, organisation and participation as sites of learning, whereas post-apartheid educational discourse increasingly privileged performance, efficiency, productivity and human-capital development. He also insists that class analysis and meaningful community participation cannot be replaced by formally equal rights or technocratic consultation. The DDC therefore treats democratic association itself as adult education and draws on participatory research in which communities help define, direct, own and use knowledge. It also keeps political economy in view: ownership, labour, unemployment, precarity and the distribution of material resources shape what participation can achieve and which possibilities are available.

The DDC combines several complementary lenses:

- **Vally and South African popular education:** organisation, struggle and democratic participation are educative; members should produce and control knowledge rather than become recipients of expert programmes.
- **Bookchin's dialectical naturalism and social ecology:** actuality does not exhaust possibility; hierarchy, not inequality alone, is a central problem; human and ecological flourishing are inseparable.
- **Moane's liberation psychology:** domination operates through structures, relationships and subjectivity; patriarchy, colonialism and trauma must be addressed without psychologising structural injustice or turning associations into therapy.
- **Rancière:** intellectual equality is a starting presumption. Expertise can contribute to inquiry but cannot justify permanent political superiority.
- **Therborn:** institutions teach what exists, what is valuable and what is possible; they can qualify people for action while subjecting them to roles. The DDC should qualify people for self-government without producing permanent governors.
- **Bourdieu's doxa:** democratic inquiry must surface assumptions that have become too ordinary to be argued about—who is expected to speak, which language counts, what a competent member looks like, and which forms of authority feel natural.
- **Social psychology:** sustained cooperative contact, dual and multiple identities, collective efficacy, protected dissent, psychological safety and fair procedures can improve democratic relations, but interpersonal harmony must never be mistaken for structural justice.
- **Ostrom and commons governance:** durable self-government depends on affected people being able to make and revise context-sensitive rules, with monitoring, proportionate sanctions, accessible conflict resolution and nested cooperation.
- **Allen's critique of progress:** the DDC can pursue emancipation and improvement without treating history as a universal ladder from traditional to modern, informal to formal or local to expert.

These traditions converge on a set of institutional commitments. Equality is presumed rather than bestowed. Difference need not become hierarchy. Delegated authority remains reviewable. Organisational knowledge should circulate rather than accumulate in indispensable people. Dissent is a democratic resource. Mutual aid provides material support for participation but does not substitute for redistribution or public provision. Accessibility is part of democracy itself, with universal design, reasonable accommodation, multimodal participation and supported decision-making built into governance. Disabled people should shape the rules that affect them, and privacy should protect people without turning disability or institutional failure into secrecy.

The DDC is also **prefigurative**: ordinary practices should make democratic alternatives materially experienceable now. A rotating chair, shared financial literacy, a multilingual meeting or a mutual-aid arrangement does more than express a value; it demonstrates that another relation is possible. The framework therefore evaluates what organisations actually enact, not only what their constitutions profess.

Its institutional architecture can be expressed as:

**people → associations → federation**

People are the source of democratic authority; associations are the primary sites of self-government; and federation enables autonomous associations to cooperate at larger scales through powers delegated upward rather than imposed downward. **Commons governance runs within and between these structures**, covering the shared resources, knowledge and capacities they create and steward together.

The future constitution generator should not produce a single ideal constitution. It should ask **non-negotiable democratic questions while allowing locally variable answers**. A burial society, civic, savings club, faith community, worker association, student formation or cooperative may use different offices, meeting forms, contribution systems and customary practices. Each should nevertheless be able to answer who belongs, who decides, how authority is delegated and reviewed, how records and resources are controlled, how participation is made accessible, how conflict and exclusion are handled, how dissent is protected, and how the organisation can revise itself.

The DDC must remain criticisable. Its own concepts of democracy, development, hierarchy, community and commons are historically situated. Its success should therefore be judged not by whether associations conform to a DDC template, but by whether they become more capable of understanding, questioning and remaking the conditions of their common life without becoming dependent on the DDC platform or its experts. Self-organisation should strengthen, not displace, collective capacity to contest wider inequalities and hold public institutions to their obligations.

## Part I — Analysis and Rationale {#part-i}

This part develops the historical, legal, theoretical and institutional case for the Democratic Development Commons. It provides the reasoning from which the proposed public-facing framework in Part II is derived.

## 1. Why a Democratic Development Commons? {#part-i-1}

### 1.1 The problem is not an absence of organisation

South Africa has never lacked associational life. Trade unions, civics, burial societies, savings clubs and stokvels, churches and faith communities, mutual-aid societies, political education groups, student organisations, neighbourhood organisations, cultural associations, sports clubs and political movements have long provided ways of organising collective life. The problem is therefore not that ordinary people need to be taught association by experts.

The problem is that associational life is repeatedly pulled toward forms that separate **governance from participation, education from action, expertise from lived knowledge, and administration from democratic control**. Organisations that begin as member-led formations can become dependent on founders, professional staff, external funders, political patrons, technical specialists or bureaucratic routines. Participation can become consultation. Education can become training. Members can become beneficiaries or clients. Constitutions can become compliance documents rather than working democratic agreements.

The DDC addresses this organisational problem directly.

### 1.2 The post-apartheid educational rupture

Vally's account of South African People's Education and Workers' Education is central to the framework. He describes a liberation tradition in which education had a purpose broader than labour-market preparation: critical understanding, democratic citizenship, social justice and participation in changing society. People's Education was simultaneously educational movement, political mobilisation and alternative philosophy. Freirean pedagogy interacted with grassroots organisation, and education became part of building alternative governance.

Workers' Education demonstrates the point even more concretely. Shop-steward councils, community forums, grievances, strikes, solidarity actions and workplace occupations were not merely contexts in which prior learning was applied; they were themselves sites of learning and knowledge production. Workers learned to analyse conditions, deliberate, organise and challenge leadership through participation. Vally describes independent unions as "laboratories of democracy".

The political transition changed this ecology. Vally argues that civil-society actors and the language of People's Education became marginal while attention shifted from social engagement and democratising power relations toward performance, outcomes, cost-effectiveness and economic competitiveness. Workers' education increasingly became associated with productivity and narrow human-capital conceptions of skills, while union bureaucratisation and weakening internal democracy further eroded its earlier role. He also identifies the loss of social-class analysis and meaningful community participation: formally available rights and consultation processes do not equal power when class, race and spatial inequality shape who can be heard and act. These inequalities are also political-economic: ownership, labour relations, unemployment, precarity and unequal control of resources structure the field in which participation occurs.

The DDC therefore does not propose an adult-education programme to accompany organisations. It proposes **democratic association itself as a form of adult education**, with community knowledge and participatory inquiry treated as sources of analysis rather than inputs to expert-designed programmes. Its purpose is not only better internal governance but greater collective capacity to act on the social conditions within which associations exist.

### 1.3 The purpose is reconstruction, not restoration

The framework should not romanticise anti-apartheid organisation. Earlier movements contained hierarchy, patriarchy, authoritarianism, exclusion and strategic errors alongside profound democratic innovation. Vally explicitly distinguishes reflective recovery of past possibilities from nostalgia that turns history into unquestionable tradition.

The DDC therefore adopts a method of **critical inheritance**:

- recover practices and memories that developed collective agency;
- analyse the hierarchies and exclusions those practices also contained;
- understand the changed material and political conditions of the present;
- experiment with adapted institutions;
- document consequences;
- revise the framework in response.

The same method applies to commons governance, cooperative traditions and South African mutual-aid forms. None is the template. All are resources for situated institution-building.

## 2. What the Democratic Development Commons Is {#part-i-2}

The DDC is best understood as a **constitutional-pedagogical framework for voluntary democratic association**.

It is constitutional because it concerns how people constitute a collective: who belongs, who decides, which powers are delegated, how resources are governed, how decisions are reviewed, and how the association changes or ends.

It is pedagogical because these arrangements continually produce learning. They teach members what kinds of knowledge matter, who is competent, whether disagreement is safe, whether authority can be questioned, and whether collective action can change conditions.

It adopts a **commons orientation**: shared resources and knowledge are governed for collective purposes rather than private enrichment, and associations can pool capacities without being absorbed into a central authority.

It is developmental because it seeks expansion and transformation of collective capacities and conditions. But it rejects developmentalism: there is no universal ladder from "traditional" to "modern" association and no institutional endpoint embodied by Europe, professional NGOs or the DDC itself.

It is also **prefigurative**. Democratic relations are not deferred to a future society; they are practised, tested and revised in present institutions. A shared budget, rotating chair, accessible meeting or mutual-aid arrangement is both practical organisation and evidence that relations commonly treated as necessary can be organised differently. Prefiguration therefore concerns enacted relations, not ideological purity.

The DDC is therefore **not**:

- a new legal form that automatically replaces existing South African law;
- a model constitution to be copied identically by every association;
- an accreditation system for deciding which organisations are sufficiently democratic;
- an NGO method for "empowering" deficient communities;
- a political doctrine that members must accept before associating;
- a therapeutic programme;
- a claim that consensus is inherently more democratic than voting;
- a requirement that informal or culturally embedded associations professionalise;
- a substitute for public institutions or an argument that collective self-organisation absolves the state of its public responsibilities.

It is a framework through which associations can make democratic commitments explicit, translate them into rules and practices, and periodically examine whether their own institutions reproduce or reduce domination. Democratic self-organisation can meet needs directly, but it can also strengthen collective demands for redistribution, public goods and accountable public institutions.

## 3. South African Constitutional, Legal and Regulatory Landscape {#part-i-3}

### 3.1 Constitutional freedom and substantive equality

The DDC begins from constitutional freedom rather than administrative permission. Section 18 protects freedom of association; section 17 protects peaceful assembly; sections 9 and 10 establish equality and dignity; section 14 protects privacy; section 29 includes adult basic education; sections 30 and 31 protect language, culture and the formation of cultural, religious and linguistic associations; section 32 protects access to information needed to exercise or protect rights; and section 24 establishes environmental rights. Disability is expressly listed in section 9 as a prohibited ground of unfair discrimination.

This is a strong constitutional base because it combines associational freedom with substantive equality. Its limitation is institutional: constitutional rights do not themselves tell a savings club how to review a treasurer, a burial society how to accommodate a disabled member, a student organisation how to protect dissent, a church-based association how to review delegated authority, or a civic how to prevent a committee from becoming politically substitutive. Rights provide the floor and direction; usable democratic institutions must still be designed.

### 3.2 Common-law voluntary associations: autonomy with legal uncertainty

South African common law allows a voluntary association to arise from agreement among its members without statutory incorporation. A written constitution is highly advisable but is not always constitutive. Courts continue to recognise that an association may acquire juristic personality as a universitas personarum where, in substance, it has perpetual succession and can hold rights, obligations and property separately from its changing membership.

This flexibility is a major strength for the DDC: it permits low-cost association and substantial constitutional self-government. The weakness is that the law is dispersed across common-law doctrine and case law. Poorly drafted rules can create uncertainty about personality, property, liability and representation, while banks, funders and counterparties may demand registrations that the common law itself does not require. The generator should therefore make legal-personality choices explicit without presenting registration as the source of association.

### 3.3 The Nonprofit Organisations Act: enabling framework, not democratic guarantee

The Nonprofit Organisations Act 71 of 1997 states an enabling purpose: creating an environment in which nonprofit organisations can flourish and providing an administrative and regulatory framework. Registration can improve public recognition, funding access and administrative legibility, and the Act specifies baseline founding-document and reporting requirements.

The framework is no longer simply voluntary in every case. Amendments effective from 1 April 2023 require registration for NPOs that make donations to persons or organisations outside South Africa or provide humanitarian, charitable, religious, educational or cultural services outside the Republic. Other qualifying NPOs may still apply voluntarily. This nuance should replace any blanket statement on the DDC website that NPO registration is wholly voluntary.

Its central democratic limitation is that compliance is not member democracy. An organisation can file reports and satisfy statutory clauses while remaining founder-controlled, inaccessible or opaque to its own members. Anti-money-laundering reforms also increase the importance of governance and disclosure, but can pull small organisations toward compliance-heavy administration. A further General Laws (Anti-Money Laundering and Combating Terrorism Financing) Amendment Bill, B15-2026, was introduced in May 2026 and underwent public hearings in August 2026; as at 18 October 2026 it should still be treated as proposed law, not as an enacted amendment.

### 3.4 Non-profit companies: strong personality, possible corporate drift

A non-profit company under the Companies Act offers clear separate legal personality, asset protection rules and a familiar framework for contracts, banking and external accountability. It can be formed with or without members.

That flexibility is also a weakness from a DDC perspective. An NPC can be legally compliant without meaningful member sovereignty, and a board-centred corporate form can become the default even where a member association would be more appropriate. The generator should therefore ask first whether members are meant to be the constitutional source of authority; if so, corporate documents should be configured to preserve that relationship rather than quietly replacing it with director sovereignty.

### 3.5 Co-operatives: the strongest existing statutory democratic bridge

The Co-operatives Act 14 of 2005 is unusually compatible with DDC principles. It recognises self-help, self-reliance, self-responsibility, democracy, equality and social responsibility, and incorporates international co-operative principles including voluntary and open membership, democratic member control, member economic participation, autonomy, education and training, co-operation among co-operatives and concern for community.

This is a major legal strength that the DDC should harness rather than duplicate. Its limits are scope and implementation. Not every mutual-aid or civic association is an enterprise, formal registration can impose unnecessary burdens on small groups, and statutory democratic principles do not guarantee democratic practice. The generator should offer a co-operative legal module where member-owned economic activity makes the form appropriate, while preserving lighter associational forms where it does not.

### 3.6 Mutual finance, burial societies and community financial self-organisation

South African financial law contains important recognition of self-organised savings and mutual-support groups. Banks Act exemptions recognise specified common-bond groups, including stokvels and other qualifying savings arrangements, outside ordinary banking regulation subject to conditions; financial-services exemptions have also recognised stokvels and burial societies in defined circumstances. These regimes show that law can recognise vernacular and community financial self-organisation without simply converting every group into a bank.

The weakness is fragmentation. Savings clubs, stokvels, burial societies and related mutual-aid forms can sit across common law, banking exemptions, financial-sector rules, insurance-related regulation and, in some cases, the Friendly Societies Act. Recent scholarship on burial societies warns that treating them primarily as funeral-insurance arrangements can misrecognise their sui generis contractual, cultural and mutual-aid character. The DDC should therefore distinguish the social constitution of the association from activity-specific financial regulation and alert groups when a chosen activity crosses into a regulated domain.

### 3.7 Equality, accessibility, information and privacy

The Promotion of Equality and Prevention of Unfair Discrimination Act 4 of 2000 (PEPUDA) gives substantive content to equality. Section 9 is especially important for disability: unfair discrimination includes denying supporting or enabling facilities, failing to eliminate obstacles that restrict equal opportunities, and failing to take reasonable steps to accommodate disabled people. This means accessibility is not merely good organisational etiquette; in relevant circumstances it is part of the legal duty not to discriminate.

The Promotion of Access to Information Act 2 of 2000 (PAIA) provides a route to information held by another person where that information is required for the exercise or protection of rights. POPIA, conversely, protects personal information and establishes conditions for lawful processing. The DDC should treat these as complementary rather than contradictory: institutional decisions, mandates and stewardship should be transparent, while personal information should be minimised and protected. Privacy should protect people, not create secrecy around power.

Where associations employ people, the Employment Equity Act and disability-related codes add workplace duties, including reasonable accommodation. NPO registration, legal personality, public-benefit organisation approval and section 18A donation deductibility are separate questions; one status does not automatically confer the others. Associations that provide formal education or accredited programmes may trigger separate education and quality-assurance requirements. Property, tax, fundraising, labour, financial-services, health, safety and other statutes may likewise apply by activity. No single constitution can safely pre-encode every regulatory pathway.

### 3.8 Disability law: strong principles, incomplete domestication

South Africa ratified the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD) and its Optional Protocol in 2007. The White Paper on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities adopts a rights-based approach centred on participation, universal design, accessibility and reasonable accommodation. Yet the White Paper is policy rather than a comprehensive disability statute, and the domestic legal framework remains fragmented.

The unfinished character of the framework is visible in South African Law Reform Commission Project 148 on domestication of the CRPD. Discussion Paper 163 proposes law reform and a draft disability-rights bill, and consultation was still continuing in 2026. The DDC should therefore implement CRPD-aligned practice now rather than wait for comprehensive legislation, while keeping its legal guidance updateable as law reform proceeds.

### 3.9 A DDC legal strategy: law as floor, resource and constraint

The DDC should neither romanticise informality nor equate formal registration with democratic legitimacy. It should use law in three ways: as a rights floor that associations must not contract below; as a resource that can provide personality, asset protection, tax treatment, financial legitimacy or enforceable safeguards; and as a constraint that becomes relevant when an association chooses regulated activities.

The practical rule for the future generator is therefore layered legal modularity. Every association receives a democratic constitution layer. Additional modules are activated by legal form and activity: common-law legal personality, NPO registration, NPC status, co-operative status, shared property, employment, cross-border activity, financial pooling, insurance-like benefits, tax status, public-benefit status or other regulated functions. The system should explain why each legal clause appears and distinguish law from DDC recommendation.

Law should set the floor of rights and accountability; the constitution should make democracy practicable above that floor.

The detailed legal matrix should be maintained separately as an updateable resource for the constitution generator, rather than embedded as a second summary of the analysis above.

## 4. An Early Inspiration: Swedish Voluntary-Association Practice {#part-i-4}

The project was initially inspired in part by Swedish association practice. It has since moved well beyond that starting point into a distinct framework grounded primarily in South African associational traditions, constitutional conditions and the wider bodies of thought examined in this paper. Swedish voluntary-association practice therefore remains relevant as one historical and comparative source rather than as the model from which the DDC is derived.

The Swedish tradition known as the *ideell förening*—a voluntary or non-profit association—is useful because it demonstrates a durable and comparatively lightweight approach to associational self-government. Swedish Tax Agency guidance describes a legal-person association as one that has adopted governing rules and chosen a board capable of representing it, with three common basic functions: member decision-making, delegated executive work and independent review.

For the DDC, the important lesson is the **grammar** rather than the Swedish institutional culture as a whole:

- a declared common purpose;
- a member body with ultimate constitutional authority;
- written rules that members can understand and amend;
- a board exercising delegated, not sovereign, authority;
- independent review of those who administer the association;
- procedures for membership, meetings, finances, amendment and dissolution.

These features offer a useful counterpoint to professionalised NGO governance in which boards can become remote legal custodians, members may not exist at all, and "beneficiaries" have no constitutional authority.

The Swedish contribution must nevertheless be decolonised in Allen's sense. Sweden is not further along a universal path of associational progress. Its practices emerged from a particular social history. South African associations should adopt, adapt or reject specific features according to their purposes and conditions.

The correct relationship is therefore:

**comparative learning without developmental ranking.**

## 5. South African Associational Resources {#part-i-5}

### 5.1 People's and Workers' Education

The DDC treats People's Education and Workers' Education as institutional precedents because they collapsed the distinction between learning and collective action. Their most important legacy is not a syllabus but an organisational proposition: **people can become more capable democratic actors through participation in organisations that make analysis, deliberation, accountability and action part of everyday practice**.

This implies that meetings, campaigns, research, budgeting and conflict resolution should be designed to distribute competence rather than merely achieve efficient outcomes. Vally's account of participatory action research adds a further requirement: communities should be able to help define questions, direct inquiry, own or control its use, interpret findings and deploy knowledge in their own struggles. Collaboration with academics or specialists should enlarge that agency rather than substitute for it. Participation should also remain connected to material questions: who controls resources, how benefits and burdens are distributed, and when collective action should seek redistribution or public provision rather than adapt to scarcity. **Democratising decision-making and redistributing material power are related but distinct tasks:** people may gain a stronger voice without gaining meaningful control over the resources and conditions that shape their lives.

### 5.2 Indigenous, vernacular and movement-based associational traditions

The DDC should begin from South Africa's rich indigenous, vernacular and movement-based traditions of association, while avoiding the claim that all such forms share a single origin or cultural logic. South Africans have long created collective institutions outside conventional NGO and corporate forms. Trade unions, civics, burial societies, savings clubs, stokvels, churches and faith communities, mutual-aid societies, political education groups, student organisations and neighbourhood organisations have all generated practical forms of solidarity, accountability, representation, learning, pooled resources and collective obligation. These traditions are diverse and sometimes internally contradictory; they should be treated as a field of institutional knowledge rather than collapsed into a single supposedly indigenous model.

Their common lesson is that sophisticated governance often develops through practice before it is expressed in professional or legal language. The DDC should therefore ask what capacities a form already contains, what problems its members have already solved, and what forms of domination or exclusion have also become normalised within it. The task is critical inheritance: preserve useful social knowledge, make power visible, and create procedures through which practices can be challenged and revised.

### 5.3 Mutual aid, savings and burial traditions

Burial societies, savings clubs, stokvels and other mutual-aid formations show how people create durable reciprocal institutions around shared material needs without waiting for external programme design. Contribution cycles, pooled resources, regular meetings, reciprocal obligations, sanctions, emergency support and shared custody arrangements demonstrate forms of governance that conventional development practice can easily misrecognise as merely informal.

The DDC should learn from reciprocity, trust, social embeddedness and member control without romanticising them. Strong cohesion can also produce gatekeeping, conformity, punitive sanctions, exclusion of people seen as unreliable, gendered care burdens or pressure to conceal hardship. Democratic adaptation should therefore make voice, financial transparency, hardship, exclusion, conflict, leadership and review more explicit without stripping these associations of their social and cultural purposes.

### 5.4 Civics, churches, student organisations and neighbourhood formations

Civics, churches and faith-based associations, student organisations, neighbourhood groups and other community formations combine identity, moral obligation, place, representation, care, political participation and collective action in different proportions. Their practices should not be flattened into a generic NGO constitution. A DDC generator should be capable of retaining culturally meaningful offices, meeting forms, ceremonies, terminology and patterns of mutual obligation while testing them against democratic questions: who can belong, who can speak, how leaders are reviewed, whether gender, age, property, clerical status or educational status determines authority, and whether dissent can occur without expulsion from the social community on which a member depends.

These traditions also demonstrate that association can be simultaneously social, educational and political. A church committee may organise care; a student formation may produce political education; a civic may negotiate with the state; a neighbourhood group may steward shared space. The DDC should allow these overlapping purposes rather than requiring organisations to resemble narrowly specialised service providers.

### 5.5 Trade unions, worker organisations and political education formations

South African worker and political education traditions contribute mandates, report-back, shop-floor and local representation, collective study, organiser development and learning through struggle. They also provide warnings about bureaucratisation, leadership insulation, professionalisation and the substitution of staff or office-bearers for rank-and-file agency. DDC-aligned organisations should therefore treat the circulation of organising knowledge, the reviewability of representatives and the ability of members to challenge leadership as core constitutional concerns.

## 6. Development Without Developmentalism {#part-i-6}

### 6.1 The problem with progress

Amy Allen's *The End of Progress* identifies a central danger for any framework using the language of development. Critical theory can oppose domination while still relying on a backward-looking narrative in which history is understood as progressive learning culminating in "our" modern institutions and standards of reason. Such narratives have been entangled with colonial claims that some societies are advanced while others represent earlier stages. The critique does not require abandoning forward-looking aspirations for emancipation or improvement; it rejects the claim that history has already validated one civilisation's path as the universal measure of progress.

The DDC therefore rejects developmental scales of the form:

traditional → modern

informal → formal

local knowledge → expert knowledge

community group → professional organisation

small → large

less rational → more rational

No movement along these axes is inherently democratic development.

A small association that deliberately remains local may be more appropriate to its purpose than one that expands. Oral practice may be more accessible than formal written procedure in one context; in another, written rules may be essential protection against arbitrary power. Professional expertise may solve a technical problem while simultaneously creating dependency. The democratic question is not "which form is more advanced?" but **what relations, capacities and possibilities does this form produce here?**

### 6.2 A DDC definition of development

For the DDC:

**Development is the open-ended expansion and transformation of the social, material, ecological and democratic conditions through which people can exercise equal standing, develop collective capacities and pursue forms of flourishing they can contest and revise together.**

Three qualifications are essential.

First, equality is not the destination of a developmental ladder. It is a normative starting presumption governing how people encounter one another.

Second, development has no predetermined institutional endpoint. Possibilities are discovered and tested in practice.

Third, the standards by which development is judged remain open to criticism. The DDC must be capable of asking what its own concepts make visible and what they obscure.

## 7. Dialectical Naturalism: Potentiality Without Teleology {#part-i-7}

Bookchin's dialectical naturalism provides a way of refusing the idea that present reality exhausts what is possible. It treats reality developmentally: existing forms contain potentials that may remain unrealised. This supports a central DDC practice of examining not only "what is" but also what capacities, relationships and institutions could become possible under altered conditions.

The framework should nevertheless use this idea carefully. Allen's critique means that potentiality cannot become a new teleology in which the DDC claims to know the proper path of social development in advance.

A democratic, non-stadial version of potentiality asks:

- What possibilities can participants identify in the present situation?
- What capacities already exist but are blocked, isolated or monopolised?
- What contradictions reveal that an existing arrangement is unstable or incomplete?
- What different futures could plausibly emerge?
- Whose conception of flourishing informs the judgment that one possibility is preferable?
- How will action create new conditions that cannot be fully predicted beforehand?

The DDC therefore replaces a linear developmental model with an iterative one:

**existing conditions → multiple possible developments → collective experiment → consequences → reflection → newly visible possibilities**

This is a dialectical conception of development without a claim that history necessarily moves toward one final form.

## 8. Social Ecology: Hierarchy as a Primary Analytic Category {#part-i-8}

Bookchin's social ecology argues that ecological domination is inseparable from social domination. The attempt to dominate nonhuman nature develops within social worlds already organised through hierarchy, command and subordination. The DDC therefore needs a stronger analytic category than inequality alone.

**Hierarchy** refers here to durable relations in which difference becomes organised as superiority and inferiority, command and obedience, or entitlement and subordination.

This distinction matters because not all differences are domination:

- expertise is not automatically domination;
- responsibility is not automatically domination;
- age difference is not automatically domination;
- coordination is not automatically domination;
- dependence is not automatically domination.

The democratic issue is whether differences are made reviewable, reciprocal and limited, or whether they become permanent claims to rule.

### 8.1 Complementarity rather than sameness

The DDC does not seek a flat society in which everyone performs identical roles. It seeks **complementarity**: differentiated capacities and responsibilities that enlarge collective capability without being converted into rank.

A member who understands accounting may temporarily carry greater financial responsibility. That should create a duty to explain, document and share knowledge rather than a permanent claim to authority. A person with legal expertise can interpret law without acquiring unilateral power to decide political strategy. An experienced elder can contribute memory and judgement without the association presuming that age alone confers command.

### 8.2 Ecology is not an added "environmental" topic

Because humans are part of nature rather than external managers of it, ecological consequences should enter the ordinary governance of the commons. Associations should ask how their activities affect land, water, energy, waste, biodiversity, mobility, care and future generations. Ecological responsibility is therefore a dimension of shared-resource governance, procurement, mutual aid and development, not a separate programme reserved for environmental organisations.

## 9. Moane: Structural, Relational and Psychological Liberation {#part-i-9}

Geraldine Moane's liberation psychology provides the bridge between structural domination and lived experience. Her work links patriarchal and colonial systems to psychological patterns associated with hierarchy and identifies liberation processes at personal, interpersonal and political levels.

This is crucial because the DDC must avoid two errors.

The first is **psychologising structural problems**: treating poverty, exclusion or racialised power as deficits in confidence, resilience, aspiration or motivation.

The second is **structural reductionism**: treating fear, shame, internalised inferiority, interpersonal hostility, trauma and relational patterns as politically irrelevant because the "real" problem is structural.

The DDC therefore treats domination as operating across interacting levels:

**structures ↔ institutions ↔ relationships ↔ subjectivity**

Democratic practice has to address all four without reducing one to another.

### 9.1 Trauma-aware, not therapeuticised

Associations may contain members whose experience includes violence, humiliation, coercion, exclusion, displacement or institutional betrayal. These histories can affect trust, conflict, participation and responses to authority.

The association should therefore be trauma-aware in its design: voluntary participation, predictable procedures, meaningful choice, protection from retaliation, alternatives to unsafe face-to-face mediation, clear boundaries, privacy and the ability to leave.

But an association is not automatically a therapeutic space. It should not diagnose members, require disclosure, interpret dissent as pathology or assume political participation will heal trauma. Where specialised support is needed, democratic governance should recognise the boundary between collective association and professional care.

### 9.2 Patriarchy and colonialism as organisational questions

Patriarchy and colonialism are not merely historical topics for educational sessions. Their effects may appear inside the organisation through who is expected to care, record, clean, host, speak, mediate, control money, represent the group or defer to elders and professionals.

A DDC association therefore asks not merely whether its constitution prohibits discrimination, but **how gendered, racialised, linguistic, classed and colonial relations are reproduced through everyday organisational labour and authority**. This includes who defines legitimate knowledge, which languages and credentials carry authority, whose history becomes organisational memory and whether community experience must be translated into professional language before it counts.

## 10. Disability: Access, Interdependence and Democratic Agency {#part-i-10}

Disability is not one protected characteristic among many; it tests the DDC's claims about equality, hierarchy, interdependence and the material conditions of participation. An association can be formally open while being designed around an unstated "normal member" who can travel easily, sit for long periods, process discussion quickly, use standard digital interfaces and communicate in dominant sensory and linguistic modes.

### 10.1 Formal equality is not democratic access

Identical treatment can reproduce exclusion. The relevant question is whether members can exercise rights and influence on substantively equal terms. PEPUDA already reflects this logic by treating failures to remove disability-related barriers or reasonably accommodate disabled people as potential unfair discrimination. DDC governance should therefore design for access before individuals are forced to request exceptions.

### 10.2 Universal design and reasonable accommodation

Universal design should shape ordinary meetings, documents, websites, venues, communications and voting systems. Reasonable accommodation remains necessary because no design anticipates every person or situation. The duty is twofold: anticipate common access needs collectively and respond to individual needs without treating accommodation as charity or lesser membership.

### 10.3 Participation is multimodal

Participation should not be equated with fast speech in a live meeting. Depending on context, legitimate modes can include speech, writing, signed and supported communication, asynchronous contributions, accessible digital participation, proxy or delegated mechanisms where democratically appropriate, and additional processing time. Access also includes physical, sensory, cognitive, neurodivergent and episodic disability, chronic illness, fatigue and pain; duration, pace, breaks, notice, deadlines and transport are therefore governance questions.

### 10.4 Agency, support and supported decision-making

Needing support does not reduce political standing. People routinely exercise agency through interpreters, assistants, technology and relationships. Where support is needed to understand information, communicate or decide, the aim should be expression of the member's own will and preferences rather than substitution by another person.

### 10.5 Nothing about us without us

Disabled people should help shape accessibility rules, resources, support and safeguarding rather than merely receive accommodations designed by others. Associations with substantial disability-related purposes should create structural routes for disabled members to set agendas and exercise oversight.

### 10.6 Privacy without secrecy or shame

Access systems should usually ask for functional needs rather than unnecessary diagnoses or histories. Personal disability information should be protected, while accessibility failures, procedures and resource allocation remain open to democratic scrutiny. Privacy protects people; it should not make disability or institutional failure unspeakable.

### 10.7 Interdependence without paternalism

Disability exposes the fiction of complete self-sufficiency. Mutual aid can make interdependence visible, but it becomes paternalistic when helpers control the support or the person receiving it. The principle is **support without political subordination**.

### 10.8 Disability as a social-ecological and doxic test

Ableism can organise bodily, sensory, cognitive and psychological difference into hierarchy. It also reveals doxa: supposedly neutral organisations embody assumptions about the normal body, mind, pace and mode of participation. **Accessibility is therefore part of the institutional form democracy takes when human variation is treated as ordinary.**

## 11. Rancière: Equality as the Starting Presumption {#part-i-11}

*The Ignorant Schoolmaster* supplies a necessary discipline against emancipatory paternalism. Rancière's challenge is that educational hierarchy can reproduce itself even when the educator intends liberation: the knowledgeable teacher explains the world to the ignorant learner and thereby continually verifies the learner's dependence on explanation. This does not replace Freirean or Vally-style critical inquiry; it disciplines such inquiry against the assumption that the educator possesses emancipation on behalf of others.

The DDC should therefore avoid language that subtly casts people as deficient recipients:

- "giving people a voice";
- "empowering communities";
- "teaching people to think critically";
- "building capacity" when this implies that capacity is absent until supplied externally.

A better premise is:

**people enter democratic association as thinking beings whose intelligence and experience already matter. Democratic organisation creates conditions in which capacities can be exercised, connected, tested and extended.**

### 11.1 Equality and development are compatible

Rancière does not require pretending that everyone possesses the same knowledge, resources or practiced skills. The DDC distinguishes **equal intellectual and political standing** from **unequal opportunities to exercise capacities**.

This yields a key formulation:

**Equality is the starting presumption of democratic relations; development concerns transforming the conditions in which equal human capacities can be exercised.**

People are therefore not "underdeveloped". Institutions can under-distribute information. Procedures can suppress participation. Social conditions can block education. Roles can monopolise skills. Development addresses these conditions rather than ranking human beings.

### 11.2 The "third thing" and collective inquiry

Rancière also suggests a useful practice: participants and facilitators can encounter a "third thing" - a text, object, map, account, budget, constitution, photograph, ecological condition or practical problem - rather than positioning one person as the authoritative interpreter of another's experience.

A DDC learning process can therefore place the object of inquiry in common:

- What do we observe?
- What can we establish?
- What differs in our interpretations?
- What evidence would we need?
- What assumptions are we making?
- What could we change?

The facilitator organises inquiry and attention without claiming a monopoly on intelligence.

## 12. Therborn: Ideology in Organisational Practice {#part-i-12}

Göran Therborn helps the DDC analyse ideology without reducing it to propaganda or false beliefs. Ideology is involved in how people become subjects able to inhabit social arrangements and understand their places within them. It helps answer three questions:

**What exists? What is good? What is possible?**

Every association supplies practical answers to these questions whether or not it uses political language.

A meeting in which only trained staff prepare agendas communicates that expertise exists in staff rather than members. A budget that treats community labour as free but consultants as payable communicates what kinds of work have value. A constitution that gives a board indefinite discretion communicates what forms of authority are considered necessary. A facilitator who always explains rather than asks communicates who is expected to know.

Ideology therefore resides in **practice, routines, categories and institutional design**. People also inhabit several social positions at once—classed, gendered, racialised, generational, religious, occupational and others—so no single category should be treated as an exhaustive explanation of subjectivity or power.

### 12.1 Subjection and qualification

Therborn's concept of "subjection-qualification" is particularly useful. Institutions develop people's capacities while simultaneously fitting them into roles.

A school may qualify a learner to analyse while subjecting the learner to expert authority. A workplace may qualify workers to solve complex production problems while subjecting them to managerial control. An NGO may qualify participants to articulate needs while subjecting them to the role of beneficiary.

The DDC should deliberately create a different relation:

**qualify people for self-government without subjecting them to permanent governors.**

Every organisational skill therefore carries a democratic question:

- Does bookkeeping create one indispensable treasurer, or broader financial literacy?
- Does facilitation create a permanent facilitator, or more people able to facilitate?
- Does research create expert ownership of knowledge, or shared capacity to investigate?
- Does legal literacy create dependence on lawyers, or better collective judgement about when legal expertise is needed?

A successful democratic institution should be capable of producing members who can challenge the institution itself. A productive contradiction appears when capacities developed for an assigned role exceed that role—for example, when organisational, legal or research skills enable members to contest the authority that originally structured their participation.

### 12.2 Ideological closure and possibility

Therborn's question "what is possible?" connects directly to dialectical naturalism. Ideology often closes potentiality by presenting contingent arrangements as necessary:

"someone has to be in charge";

"ordinary members cannot understand this";

"funders require it, so there is no alternative";

"the market is simply reality";

"this is how our culture works".

DDC inquiry asks whether necessity is genuine, legal, material, customary or merely habitual - and what experiments might reveal alternatives. This is where prefiguration becomes ideology in practice: democratic institutions can function as counter-institutional spaces in which people experience different relations as workable rather than merely hear arguments for them.

## 13. Bourdieu: Doxa and the Taken-for-Granted {#part-i-13}

Pierre Bourdieu adds a layer beneath articulated ideology. Doxa concerns the practical assumptions that are so deeply fitted to a social world that they are not experienced as positions requiring defence. They appear self-evident. Bourdieu links doxa to the fit between social structures and habitus: historically produced arrangements can be misrecognised as natural, inevitable or simply sensible.

### 13.1 Doxa is not simply ideology

Therborn helps the DDC ask what an institution teaches people to see as real, good and possible. Doxa asks an earlier question: what has not yet become a question at all? Orthodoxy and heterodoxy become possible when previously tacit assumptions enter discourse and competing answers can be stated. Democratic inquiry therefore has to notice not only disputed beliefs but the boundaries of what participants currently find thinkable.

### 13.2 Organisational doxa

Associations can carry doxic assumptions in apparently neutral routines: that leaders sit at the front; that the chair speaks most; that English is the language of serious administration; that written fluency indicates competence; that financial expertise belongs to a treasurer; that paid professional labour is more valuable than care or community labour; that meetings must be synchronous; that fast responses show intelligence; that privacy justifies withholding institutional information; that disability accommodation is exceptional; or that a larger and more professional organisation is necessarily more developed.

These assumptions are powerful precisely because members may reproduce them without explicitly believing or endorsing them. Enacted norms can therefore contradict written rules: a constitution may declare equality while interruptions, jokes, seating, deference or control of information teach members a different order. The constitution generator must ask about practices that ordinary governance templates leave unexamined.

### 13.3 Doxa, crisis and possibility

Doxa often becomes visible when practice breaks down: a treasurer leaves and nobody else can access the account; a disabled member cannot attend the AGM; a language conflict reveals who was assumed to understand; a funding crisis exposes dependency on one donor; a younger member questions a customary gender role. Such moments should not be treated only as disruptions. They can reveal the hidden assumptions that organised the previous equilibrium.

### 13.4 Reflexivity without a new expert hierarchy

The DDC must not turn doxa into a doctrine in which trained facilitators reveal the hidden truth to ordinary members. Consistent with Rancière and Allen, the task is collective problematisation: make practices describable, compare experiences, ask who benefits or bears costs, investigate history, test alternatives and remain open to being wrong.

A democratic association asks not only what it believes, but what it has never thought to question.

## 14. Social Psychology: Designing Democratic Relations {#part-i-14}

Social psychology helps explain the micro-social processes through which association becomes cooperative, conformist, conflictual or empowering. The DDC uses it to design democratic conditions, not to individualise structural problems.

### 14.1 Intergroup contact: cooperation, not proximity

Intergroup contact generally reduces prejudice, especially where participants meet with relatively equal status, common goals, cooperation and institutional support for equality. The practical lesson is that **diversity of attendance is not enough**. Associations should create sustained joint work around purposes participants help define. The same applies online: direct, repeated cooperation can extend contact across associations, but digital participation should not eliminate accessible non-digital routes.

### 14.2 Harmony is not liberation

Positive contact can coexist with structural inequality and may sometimes make injustice less visible. The DDC therefore rejects the equation **people like one another more → the relationship is more just**. It should practise relational equality while retaining the ability to name unequal labour, resource, racial, gendered or institutional relations.

Contact must also be reciprocal. Members of subordinated groups should not have to disclose trauma, educate dominant groups through personal testimony or absorb harmful encounters so that others can develop empathy. Shared tasks and common objects of inquiry can create encounter without turning suffering into curriculum.

### 14.3 Common, dual and multiple identities

A common identity can reduce intergroup bias but can also pressure minorities to suppress meaningful differences. The DDC should therefore support dual identity where useful: people can belong to the association while retaining identities and histories that matter to them. Members also hold multiple, cross-cutting identities; no person should be reduced to one category, while structurally important inequalities remain nameable.

### 14.4 Collective efficacy

Collective action becomes more likely when people share identity, perceive injustice and believe they can act effectively together. Associations should therefore create achievable opportunities to experience collective capacity and then reflect on what changed, how action contributed and what becomes possible next. This is preferable to an external actor "empowering" participants.

### 14.5 Dissent, conformity and consensus

Horizontal groups also exert conformity pressure. Apparent consensus may reflect fatigue, prestige, fear of rejection or founder status. Minority dissent can improve inquiry and innovation, so consequential decisions should permit objections, minority reasoning, delayed finalisation or reopening where appropriate. Consensus is one method, not a moral measure of unity.

### 14.6 Psychological safety as contestability

Psychological safety matters politically when members can question, admit uncertainty, report mistakes and contradict authority without predictable humiliation or retaliation. A democratic test is whether a newcomer can challenge a founder, a member can say they do not understand the finances, and an unpopular view can lose a vote without its holder losing belonging. Safety means the capacity to participate in disagreement, not freedom from disagreement.

### 14.7 Procedural justice

Decision processes should be tested for four features: **voice** (affected people can participate), **impartiality** (rules are not manipulated for favoured people), **respect** (participants are treated as having standing), and **reason-giving** (decisions can be explained). Procedural fairness cannot make domination just; it must remain connected to structural analysis.

## 15. Commons Governance: Rules That People Can Make and Revise {#part-i-15}

Elinor Ostrom's work on durable commons is valuable because it rejects the assumption that shared resources necessarily require either privatisation or central state control. Her empirical design principles include clear boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, opportunities for affected users to participate in rule modification, monitoring, graduated sanctions, accessible conflict-resolution mechanisms, recognition of self-organisation and nested enterprises.

Ostrom was explicit that these were not a universal set of identical rules. They were higher-level characteristics observed across durable systems; their concrete expression varied.

This supports a core DDC distinction:

**non-negotiable democratic questions; locally variable institutional answers.**

For example, every association needs a credible way to decide who may authorise expenditure. But the precise financial threshold, meeting frequency or combination of signatories should depend on scale, risk and purpose.

### 15.1 The commons is broader than money

DDC shared resources can include:

- money and pooled contributions;
- land, rooms and meeting spaces;
- equipment and tools;
- records and archives;
- educational materials;
- research and data;
- digital infrastructure;
- organisational knowledge;
- social networks and relationships;
- time and care labour.

Governance should make visible who creates, controls and benefits from these resources.

## 16. The DDC Institutional Architecture {#part-i-16}

The framework relates two organisational scales—association and federation—through a commons dimension, with people as the source of democratic authority. These are not a fixed ladder or separate legal forms.

### 16.1 Association

The association is the primary site of member sovereignty. People define a common purpose, adopt rules and determine how authority is delegated.

### 16.2 Commons

The commons is the shared-resource dimension of association: resources created, contributed or stewarded for common purposes, including organisational memory and knowledge as well as material assets. Commons can be governed within one association or jointly across several associations.

### 16.3 Federation

Federation enables autonomous associations to cooperate at scales larger than one association. Shared functions might include legal support, education, research, digital infrastructure, procurement, communications, publications, specialist advice or collective representation.

Authority should move **upward through explicit delegation**, not downward from a national centre. Federated bodies should exercise only the powers entrusted to them, and delegates should remain accountable to constituent associations.

The institutional aim is:

**coordination without domination; scale without unnecessary centralisation.**

## 17. Organisation as Popular Education {#part-i-17}

The DDC treats all important organisational activity as potentially educational, but internal reflection is not its endpoint. Democratic learning should strengthen the association's capacity to act on the conditions that gave rise to its work.

A treasurer's role includes making finances intelligible to members.

A secretary preserves organisational memory and makes records usable.

A chair enables deliberation rather than displaying authority.

A researcher helps members interrogate evidence.

An experienced organiser creates additional organisers rather than followers.

This yields a demanding criterion:

**an organisation should become less dependent on the permanent indispensability of particular people as its democratic capacities mature.**

This is not an argument against expertise or continuity. Some roles require specialist knowledge and long experience. The requirement is that expertise be accountable, explain itself, leave records and avoid unnecessary monopolisation.

### 17.1 Praxis: action, inquiry and renewed action

At the broadest level, DDC praxis follows a simple cycle:

**democratise the organisation → act collectively on the world → learn from the consequences → democratise and act again**

Within that cycle, collective inquiry can move through:

**experience → describe → investigate → historicise → identify relationships and contradictions → identify possibilities → deliberate as equals → act → observe consequences → reflect → revise**

Useful questions include:

- What exists, and how did it become this way?
- What relations of power sustain it and whom do they affect?
- What assumptions are we making, and who is missing from the inquiry?
- What possibilities are visible, and what action can test our understanding?
- What did the consequences teach us, including about our own organisation?

Education is therefore not a separate workshop added to organisational life. Collective action generates learning, and learning returns to both the organisation and the world it is trying to change.

## 18. Five Standing Review Practices {#part-i-18}

The DDC should convert its theory into recurring member practices. Five complementary reviews can do this. They should remain proportionate and serve action: their purpose is to identify changes the association can make internally or pursue collectively in the wider world, not to turn democratic life into continuous self-audit.

### 18.1 Hierarchy and Potentiality Review

**Hierarchy questions**

- Who currently has formal authority?
- Who has informal influence because of money, expertise, language, age, status or networks?
- Who controls information, passwords, accounts, relationships or organisational memory?
- Who performs invisible or unpaid labour?
- Which roles have become difficult to challenge or replace?
- Are differences becoming relations of superiority or dependency?

**Potentiality questions**

- What capacities are members beginning to exercise?
- Which skills could be shared more widely?
- What forms of cooperation are emerging?
- What resources could become common resources?
- What problems reveal opportunities for institutional change?
- What possibilities do different members see?
- What might current arrangements prevent us from imagining?

### 18.2 Democratic Relations Review

- Are socially separated members actually working together or merely attending the same meetings?
- Does contact occur on reasonably equal terms?
- Can members retain meaningful subgroup identities while belonging to the common association?
- Who speaks, interrupts, proposes and disagrees?
- Are minority views genuinely considered?
- Can members challenge founders and leaders without retaliation?
- What has the group demonstrably accomplished together?
- Which informal behaviours receive social approval or punishment?
- When contact goes badly, can relationships be repaired?
- Is improved interpersonal harmony concealing continued structural inequality?

### 18.3 Ideology and Doxa in Practice Review

- What does this practice teach us exists?
- What does it reward as good, competent or respectable?
- What alternatives does it make seem impossible or unserious?
- What kind of member, leader, beneficiary or expert does it produce?
- What assumptions are so taken for granted that they are not presently being argued about?
- Whose ease or normality is built into those assumptions?
- What event, conflict or exclusion would make the hidden assumption visible?

### 18.4 Genealogy and Unlearning Review

This Allen-inspired review turns critique back on the framework itself:

- Why are we describing the problem in these terms?
- Where did this category come from?
- What history of power made this institutional form appear normal?
- Are we equating formalisation, professionalisation, scale or technical expertise with progress?
- Which local or marginalised forms of knowledge become invisible under our terminology?
- Whose conception of flourishing informs our judgement?
- What would someone differently positioned question about our assumptions?

The purpose is not permanent indecision. It is **conviction without epistemic supremacy**: the ability to act while remaining corrigible.

### 18.5 Participation and Access Review

The association periodically examines whether formally available participation is substantively usable, with particular attention to disability and other material barriers.

- Which members are absent, silent, exhausted or dependent on others to access ordinary processes?
- Which participation modes are treated as normal, and which require special permission?
- Can members obtain key information in formats they can use?
- Do meeting pace, duration, deadlines, venues, transport or digital platforms exclude people?
- Are accommodation decisions controlled by the person affected or by gatekeepers?
- Is unnecessary medical or personal disclosure being demanded?
- Do disabled members have meaningful influence over accessibility priorities and budgets?
- Which barriers can be removed through universal design, and which require individual accommodation?

The review should produce concrete changes, assigned responsibilities and a date for checking whether access improved in practice.

## 19. Accountability in Three Directions {#part-i-19}

Conventional nonprofit governance often prioritises upward accountability to funders and regulators. The DDC should distinguish three directions.

### 19.1 Inward accountability

Office-bearers and delegates answer to members for decisions, finances, mandates, commitments and conduct.

### 19.2 Horizontal accountability

Associations also answer to people materially affected by their actions even when those people are not members. A housing, environmental or service-delivery campaign may affect neighbours who never joined the organisation.

### 19.3 Outward accountability

The association must comply with relevant law, registration duties, contractual commitments, tax rules, employment duties and legitimate partnership obligations.

These directions can conflict. The DDC should not pretend otherwise. External funding, for example, may create reporting requirements that consume member time or change priorities. Such tensions should become explicit subjects of governance rather than invisible administrative facts.

## 20. Application to Different South African Association Types {#part-i-20}

The DDC is a configurable grammar. Different traditions should retain their social purposes, terminology and useful practices while being tested against common democratic questions.

| **Association type** | **What to preserve** | **Key risks to test** | **Likely constitutional emphasis** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neighbourhood civic / residents' association | Place-based knowledge, mobilisation, public representation | Claiming to speak for non-members; party or property-owner capture; access barriers | Mandates, affected-community voice, conflicts of interest, representative expiry |
| Burial / bereavement society | Solidarity, culturally meaningful care, rapid support, pooled resources | Opaque discretion, gendered care, exclusion, tension between flexibility and fund sustainability | Eligibility, benefits, hardship rules, emergency authority, appeals, asset custody |
| Savings club / stokvel / mutual-finance group | Reciprocal contribution, regular meeting, trust, member control | Punitive exclusion, social pressure, entrenched leadership, unequal financial understanding | Contribution and payout rules, hardship, dual authorisation, readable statements, financial review |
| Church / faith or culturally rooted association | Community, care, ritual, durable identity | Unreviewable spiritual/customary authority, conformity pressure, gender/age hierarchy | Limits on office, member review, protected dissent, resource stewardship |
| Student / political-education group | Study, mobilisation, rapid leadership development, debate | Factional capture, ideological conformity, charisma, memory loss | Mandates, rotating facilitation, minority positions, archives, succession |
| Trade union / worker association / worker-education collective | Worker knowledge, mandates, report-back, solidarity, learning through struggle | Bureaucratisation, organiser substitution, inaccessible technical knowledge | Local mandates, shared legal literacy, recall, report-back, member-led research |
| Community learning / research collective | Inquiry, participatory research, public education | Extractive research, credential hierarchy, funder-driven agendas | Research ownership, data governance, authorship, accessible outputs, shared interpretation |
| Cooperative / member-owned enterprise | Member ownership, shared economic purpose, democratic control | Managerial authority becoming political authority; productivity displacing democracy | Reserved member decisions, remuneration transparency, manager mandates, social/ecological purpose |
| Federation | Shared scale with local autonomy | Centralisation, staff substitution, wealthier affiliates dominating | Enumerated powers, subsidiarity, mandated delegates, report-back, transparent transfers, withdrawal |

These examples are prompts, not a taxonomy of South African association. The generator should remain capable of hybrid and locally named forms.

## 21. Requirements for the Future Constitution Generator {#part-i-21}

The generator should be a **guided democratic design process**, not a boilerplate form.

### 21.1 Start with purpose and context

Ask what people are forming, what shared need or purpose brings them together, whether they pool money or assets, whether vulnerable people are affected, whether they employ people, and which legal or tax statuses or regulated activities may be relevant.

### 21.2 Distinguish fixed questions from variable answers

Every association should answer the same democratic questions without being forced into the same institutional form.

| **Democratic question** | **Examples of locally variable answers** |
|---|---|
| Who holds ultimate authority? | all members; defined member classes; constituent associations |
| How are decisions made? | majority; supermajority; consensus with fallback vote; delegated thresholds |
| How do members shape agendas and verify decisions? | proposal rights; agenda windows; approved minutes; minute reviewers; recorded minority positions |
| How is executive work done? | board; rotating committee; office-bearers; task circles |
| How is scrutiny independent? | elected reviewers; audit committee; external auditor; peer review |
| How are contributions organised? | fixed or proportional money; in-kind labour; voluntary contributions |
| How is hardship handled? | pause; reduction; emergency fund; member decision |
| How are disputes handled? | internal panel; peer mediation; external mediator; appeal to general meeting |
| How is access provided? | multilingual records; interpretation; oral explanation; accessible digital and non-digital modes |

The generator must not silently privilege the most bureaucratic answer.

### 21.3 Make power choices explicit

Whenever a clause creates an office, committee or special power, explain what is delegated, why it may be useful, how long it lasts, what must be reported, who reviews it and how it can be withdrawn. Constitution-making should itself be democratic education.

### 21.4 Build accessibility into governance

Ask how notices, meetings, voting, accessible formats, interpretation, reasonable accommodation, supported participation, privacy and access budgets work. Avoid clauses that require unnecessary medical proof or equate physical attendance with participation.

### 21.5 Treat sanctions as governance

Sanctions may protect reciprocal obligations or members from harm, but should be foreseeable, proportionate, reviewable and attentive to unequal power. The generator should distinguish inability from refusal or misconduct.

### 21.6 Give key roles educational duties

Where appropriate, require treasurers to report in member-readable form, secretaries to preserve accessible records, chairs to facilitate participation, and specialists to support succession rather than monopolise knowledge.

### 21.7 Build in review and federation options

Offer periodic constitutional and democratic review clauses, and optional federation clauses governing delegated functions, representatives, dues, shared assets, report-back and withdrawal.

### 21.8 Keep legal guidance modular

Legal guidance should be date-stamped and separate from democratic recommendations. Activate only the modules relevant to legal form and activity; distinguish enacted law from proposals; and label each clause as a **legal requirement, DDC recommendation, prudent safeguard or locally variable choice**. Maintain the legal matrix separately so the constitutional philosophy does not become obsolete when law changes.

## 22. Evaluation: What Counts as Democratic Development? {#part-i-22}

The DDC should not reproduce neoliberal evaluation by measuring only outputs, attendance, efficiency and cost. Nor should it abandon evidence.

Evaluation should combine practical outcomes with changes in collective capacity and social relations, including epistemic, relational, political, institutional and personal dimensions of participation.

Possible questions include:

**Purpose and outcomes**

Did the association materially advance its shared purpose?

**Participation**

Who participated and who remained excluded? Did material barriers decline?

**Distributed capacity**

Can more members understand finances, facilitate meetings, access records, conduct research or represent the association than before?

**Collective efficacy**

Do members have evidence that collective action can change conditions?

**Power**

Has authority become more accountable or more concentrated? Have informal hierarchies changed?

**Relations**

Are members cooperating across prior social boundaries without suppressing meaningful differences or structural inequalities?

**Dissent**

Can unpopular views be expressed and considered without retaliation?

**Mutual aid and distribution**

Are members better able to meet shared needs through reciprocal arrangements? Where relevant, has collective action changed access to, control over or distribution of material resources rather than merely helping people cope with unequal conditions?

**Public responsibility**

Where a need properly engages public obligations, has the association strengthened members' capacity to demand accountable public provision rather than silently substituting for it?

**Knowledge**

Is organisational knowledge becoming more common, or accumulating in a few people?

**Ecology**

How do activities affect shared environmental conditions and future needs?

**Reflexivity**

Has the association revised assumptions or rules because experience contradicted them?

A democratic organisation can fail at an objective and still learn. It can also achieve an objective while becoming less democratic. Evaluation must be capable of recognising both.

## 23. Risks and Safeguards {#part-i-23}

The framework should identify recurring failure modes without turning every risk into additional bureaucracy.

| **Risk** | **Safeguard** |
|---|---|
| Bureaucratisation | Proportionate records, member-readable reports and periodic rule review. |
| Founder or charismatic-leader capture | Shared facilitation, accessible information, term review and workable recall. |
| Funder or institutional capture | Disclose conditions and dependencies; require member approval for significant agreements. |
| Consensus pressure | Protect dissent, provide fallback procedures and record minority reasoning where material. |
| Professionalisation as progress | Separate expertise from political authority and test assumptions that scale or credentials equal development. |
| Cultural romanticism | Preserve locally meaningful forms while keeping gender, age, disability, dissent and membership effects contestable. |
| Imported-model romanticism | Use Swedish, cooperative and commons practices comparatively, not as superior stages or templates. |
| Therapeutic creep | Be trauma-aware without diagnosis, compelled disclosure or pathologising disagreement. |
| Legalism and compliance capture | Separate legal duties from democratic choices; use plain language and the least centralisation necessary for compliance. |
| Digital exclusion and technical capture | Provide accessible, documented, multiple participation channels and non-digital alternatives where feasible. |
| Harmony without justice | Pair intergroup cooperation with structural analysis and affected-group voice. |
| Inward proceduralism | Keep reviews proportionate and tie reflection to shared purposes, collective action and consequences beyond the organisation. |
| Privatised self-help | Use mutual aid where useful without allowing it to obscure redistribution or replace legitimate public obligations. |
| Developmentalism | Criticise concrete relations rather than ranking communities or associations along a universal scale. |
| Ableist procedural neutrality | Use universal design, reasonable accommodation, supported participation and access review. |
| Doxic closure | Treat breakdown, exclusion and conflict as opportunities to surface assumptions and test alternatives. |

## 24. Design Principles for the DDC Infrastructure {#part-i-24}

The website and later tools should embody the framework rather than merely describe it.

### 24.1 Accessible by default

Content and tools should follow universal-design principles: plain but non-patronising language, multilingual publication, semantic headings, screen-reader compatibility, keyboard access, high zoom and reflow, captions and transcripts, optional text-to-speech, accessible downloadable formats, support for South African Sign Language where feasible, and low-bandwidth/mobile use. Interaction should not depend on drag-and-drop, colour alone, precise motor control, rapid timeouts or one sensory channel. Accessibility preferences should be remembered where the user chooses, without unnecessary profiling.

### 24.2 Explain choices, not only outputs

The constitution generator should show why a clause exists, which democratic risk it addresses and what alternatives are available. Users should be able to learn constitution-making while doing it.

### 24.3 Preserve local vocabulary

The system should allow associations to use familiar names for meetings, roles, funds and practices rather than forcing all groups into NGO terminology.

### 24.4 Keep knowledge portable

Constitutions, guides, meeting templates and learning materials should be exportable in open and accessible formats. Shared knowledge should not become dependent on one proprietary platform.

### 24.5 Minimise data extraction

The platform should collect only information necessary for its functions. Organising infrastructure should not reproduce a surveillance model in which participation becomes a source of unnecessary personal data.

### 24.6 Design for revision

The framework, generated constitutions and educational content should make amendment normal. Version history can become part of organisational memory rather than an embarrassment indicating that founders "got it wrong".

## 25. Research and Development Agenda {#part-i-25}

Before the DDC is reduced to final website principles or encoded in a constitution generator, several strands should be developed further.

### 25.1 Association-type research

Document constitutions, meeting procedures, sanction practices, succession arrangements and informal norms across a purposive range of South African trade unions, civics, burial societies, savings clubs, stokvels, churches and faith-based associations, mutual-aid societies, political education groups, student organisations, neighbourhood organisations and member-owned enterprises. The objective should not be to discover "the South African model", but to identify recurring problems and locally developed solutions across a plural associational ecology.

### 25.2 Legal mapping

Maintain, as a separate updateable resource for the generator, a dated legal matrix distinguishing common-law voluntary associations, registered NPOs, non-profit companies, co-operatives, trusts, communal-property associations, stokvel/common-bond exemptions, burial-society and friendly-society regimes, tax/public-benefit status, employment duties, disability/equality duties and other activity-specific regulation. Track proposed reforms separately from enacted law. The generator should know when a provision is a democratic choice, a legal requirement, a prudent safeguard or merely one available institutional pattern.

### 25.3 Participatory testing

Test the framework with actual associations before finalisation, including disabled-led organisations and disabled members across different impairment and communication experiences. Members should identify which concepts make sense, which questions reveal useful problems, which access assumptions remain hidden, which legal explanations are usable, and which provisions reproduce professional, academic or ableist hierarchy.

### 25.4 Language development

Key concepts - commons, accountability, mandate, delegation, review, conflict of interest, federation, hierarchy, mutual aid - need careful development across South African languages rather than literal translation after English concepts are fixed.

### 25.5 Governance-pattern library

Instead of one template, the generator should maintain a library of tested constitutional patterns: contribution rules, hardship clauses, rotating facilitation, reviewer models, federation mandates, conflict procedures and accessible meeting practices. Each pattern should explain what problem it addresses and when it may be unsuitable.

### 25.6 Evaluation through use

The strongest test of the DDC will be whether associations using it become more capable of governing themselves without becoming more dependent on the DDC platform or its experts. The framework should therefore measure its own success partly through **decreasing indispensability**.

## 26. Conclusion {#part-i-26}

The Democratic Development Commons joins freedom of association, South African popular education and associational traditions, mutual aid, commons governance, social ecology, liberation psychology, decolonial critique, disability justice, social psychology and constitutional self-government around one problem: **how can people build institutions that expand collective agency without reproducing domination?**

Its answer is institutional rather than doctrinal. Members retain sovereignty; delegated power remains contestable; knowledge and organisational capacity circulate; accessibility is built into governance; shared resources are stewarded transparently; dissent remains possible; and autonomous associations can cooperate without surrendering local self-government. These practices should strengthen collective action on material and political conditions outside the association, including struggles over distribution and public responsibility. The framework also requires reflexivity: organisations must examine not only explicit rules but the ideology, doxa and informal norms their practices reproduce.

Part II offers a provisional public distillation of this analysis. The next development stage is to test and refine it with actual associations, maintain the legal guidance, and map the resulting principles to configurable constitutional questions, clauses and review practices. The resulting tools should not make organisations dependent on the DDC; their strongest measure of success will be whether associations become better able to understand, govern, question and remake themselves.

## Part II — Proposed Public Framework {#part-ii}

This part translates the analysis in Part I into a possible public structure for the Democratic Development Commons website and, later, the constitution generator. It is **a proposal, not a settled information architecture**. The five headings below inherit elements from the earlier website draft, but they should be renamed, merged, divided or expanded where participatory testing, legal development or further analysis shows that another structure works better.

The public framework should remain much shorter than Part I. Its purpose is to make the DDC usable without requiring people to master its theoretical sources. Theory should remain available as explanation and justification, while the public framework states the democratic choices that associations can put into practice.

### 1. The Idea {#part-ii-1}

#### Development begins with collaboration

People should be free to associate around shared needs, purposes and possibilities, and to govern the institutions they create. Collaboration means acting together across differences; it does not require harmony or exclude disagreement, contestation or organised challenge to power.

Democratic agency is not bestowed on passive beneficiaries by experts, organisations, markets or the state. It develops through people's collective capacity to understand their circumstances, make decisions, care for one another, steward shared resources, challenge domination, learn from action and revise the arrangements through which they live and work together. Public institutions nevertheless retain their obligations to provide rights, public goods and accountable services.

The Democratic Development Commons provides a shared framework for doing this. It draws on South Africa's diverse traditions of worker, civic, mutual-aid, educational, faith, student, neighbourhood and other voluntary organisation; on useful practices developed elsewhere; and on constitutional commitments to freedom, equality, dignity and participation.

It does not prescribe one organisational form or developmental path. Different associations can retain their own purposes, names, customs and structures while asking common democratic questions about power, participation, knowledge, resources, accountability and change.

The DDC is a voluntary framework. It does not replace South African law, create legal personality, confer registration or tax status, or remove activity-specific legal duties.

A concise public proposition could be:

> **We learn democracy by doing democracy.**

A fuller proposition could be:

> **People develop democratic capacity by acting together, learning from what they do, and retaining the power to question and remake the institutions they create.**

### 2. Foundations / Organising Principles {#part-ii-2}

The following principles are proposed as the public foundations of the DDC. Their number and wording should remain open to testing and refinement.

#### 2.1 Freedom and voluntary association

People may form, join, support or leave associations without unnecessary external control. Membership expectations, responsibilities and consequences should be understandable before people commit.

#### 2.2 Equal standing

Every member is approached as a person capable of thought, judgement and participation. Differences in knowledge, ability, experience, status or responsibility do not create superior and inferior classes of members.

#### 2.3 Collective self-government

Members retain ultimate authority over the association's purpose, constitution and major shared resources. Representatives and specialists exercise delegated powers rather than replacing member agency.

#### 2.4 Difference without domination

Democracy does not require sameness. Differences should enlarge collective capacity rather than harden into relations of command, dependency, exclusion or superiority.

#### 2.5 Substantive participation and accessibility

A formal right to participate is insufficient where language, literacy, disability, transport, cost, care, work, safety, technology or social status make participation unusable. Democratic organisation should remove barriers through universal design, reasonable accommodation and multiple ways of participating.

#### 2.6 Collective learning and inquiry

Organisation is educational. Members learn through meetings, campaigns, research, conflict, budgeting, care, practical work and reflection. Expertise should enlarge collective understanding rather than establish a monopoly over knowledge.

#### 2.7 Shared stewardship, mutual aid and ecological responsibility

Money, knowledge, spaces, equipment, time, care and other shared resources should serve agreed purposes rather than private enrichment. Associations should organise reciprocal support where appropriate and consider the ecological consequences of their activities. Mutual aid can make participation and solidarity materially possible, but it should not obscure wider inequalities, substitute for redistribution or relieve public institutions of legitimate obligations.

#### 2.8 Accountable power, dissent and review

Delegated authority must remain visible, limited, explainable and reviewable. Members need practical ways to question decisions, express minority positions, challenge leaders and seek independent scrutiny without retaliation.

#### 2.9 Autonomy and federation

Associations should control their own purposes while remaining free to cooperate. Larger structures should derive authority from constituent associations through explicit delegation rather than accumulate unnecessary power at the centre.

#### 2.10 Open-ended and reflexive development

There is no universal ladder from "traditional" to "modern" organisation. Associations should examine what their practices make possible, whose interests they serve, which assumptions have become taken for granted and what experience suggests should change.

### 3. The Framework {#part-ii-3}

The foundations become practical through commitments that associations can translate into their own constitutions and everyday practices.

#### 3.1 Begin with a shared purpose

Agree what brings people together, who is affected by the work, what members hope to accomplish, which conditions they seek to change and how the purpose may develop.

#### 3.2 Make the constitution a working agreement

Set out membership, decision-making, delegated powers, resources, accountability, conflict, amendment and closure in language and formats members can use. The constitution should be revisable rather than treated as a founding document that disappears into a file.

#### 3.3 Keep fundamental decisions with members

Identify which decisions require the membership itself and which can be delegated. Significant changes to purpose, constitutional rules, leadership powers or major shared assets should return to members.

#### 3.4 Delegate without political substitution

Give representatives enough authority to do necessary work, but define their mandate, duration, reporting duties, review and removal. Delegation should follow the recurring relationship:

**mandate → action → report → scrutiny → revision**

#### 3.5 Make participation usable

Design notices, meetings, documents, voting and communication around the people who must use them. Provide relevant language access, accessible formats, supported participation and reasonable accommodation without unnecessary personal or medical disclosure.

#### 3.6 Distribute knowledge and organisational capacity

Document important work, share skills and plan succession. Finance, technology, facilitation, external relationships and organisational memory should not become unnecessarily concentrated in indispensable individuals.

#### 3.7 Protect democratic encounter and dissent

Create sustained opportunities for people across social differences to work together on shared purposes, without pretending that structural inequalities have disappeared. Treat disagreement and minority reasoning as sources of information rather than evidence of disloyalty.

#### 3.8 Steward shared resources and practise mutual aid

Agree how money, assets, knowledge, care, spaces and other common resources are contributed, used, recorded and transferred. Distinguish hardship from misconduct and avoid quietly excluding people because they cannot meet an assumed norm. Where needs arise from wider structural conditions, ask whether the appropriate response is mutual aid, redistribution, a public claim, or some combination of these.

#### 3.9 Establish independent scrutiny

People exercising delegated authority should not be solely responsible for reviewing themselves. Members should appoint independent reviewers appropriate to the scale and risks of the association.

#### 3.10 Keep trustworthy and accessible records

Record decisions, responsibilities and financial information in forms members can inspect. Where appropriate, members should verify that meeting records accurately reflect what was decided.

#### 3.11 Address conflict and harm fairly

Distinguish disagreement from harmful conduct. Provide accessible routes for raising concerns, alternatives when powerful people are implicated, opportunities to respond, proportionate remedies and review.

#### 3.12 Protect organisational autonomy

Make major funding conditions, partnerships, political relationships and institutional dependencies visible. Members should be able to judge whether external support is redirecting priorities, speech or governance.

#### 3.13 Learn through action and periodic review

Use collective action, its consequences and reflection as sources of knowledge. Periodically examine power, democratic relations, ideology and taken-for-granted assumptions, genealogy and unlearning, participation and access, then return what is learned to action.

#### 3.14 Federate without unnecessary centralisation

Where cooperation at larger scale is useful, specify which powers remain local and which are delegated upward. Delegates should carry mandates and report back.

#### 3.15 Use law as a floor and resource

Identify the legal form and activity-specific rules relevant to the association. Meet applicable rights, registration, reporting, equality, privacy, tax, employment, financial or other duties without confusing legal compliance with democratic quality.

### 4. In Practice {#part-ii-4}

This section should show how the framework can operate without implying that every association needs the same offices.

#### 4.1 Members

Members constitute the democratic base of the association. Their rights, responsibilities, admission, suspension, exit and routes for participation should be clear.

#### 4.2 General meeting or assembly

The membership's principal decision-making forum should deal with matters reserved to members, receive reports, review delegated authority and amend governing rules. The precise name, frequency and procedure can vary.

#### 4.3 Board, committee or delegated office-bearers

A board or equivalent body carries out work between member meetings within defined powers. Its existence should not convert delegated executive responsibility into sovereignty over the association.

#### 4.4 Working groups and facilitators

Associations may create temporary or continuing groups for particular tasks. Their mandates, resources and reporting relationships should be clear, and facilitation should support participation rather than establish intellectual authority.

#### 4.5 Independent reviewers

Members appoint people who are sufficiently independent of those administering the association to review finances, mandates, procedure, conflicts of interest and other material obligations. Larger or regulated organisations may require professional audit in addition to democratic review.

#### 4.6 Constitution, records and organisational memory

Members should be able to access the governing rules, important decisions, financial information and records needed to understand the association. Organisational memory should survive leadership changes.

#### 4.7 Starting an association

A possible public checklist is:

1. Identify the people, shared purpose and people materially affected by the work.
2. Discuss membership, participation, language, disability access and material barriers before fixing procedures.
3. Identify the legal form and activity-specific duties that may apply.
4. Agree which decisions remain with members and which may be delegated.
5. Draft and collectively adopt a usable constitution.
6. Choose representatives and independent reviewers, define their mandates and plan succession.
7. Agree how money, assets, records, privacy and conflicts of interest will be handled.
8. Record and verify founding decisions and appointments.
9. Arrange banking, registrations, tax approvals or regulated permissions where required.
10. Begin the work, then review what the rules produce in practice and amend them when necessary.

#### 4.8 Five recurring democratic reviews

Associations can periodically use five short review practices:

**Hierarchy and potentiality:** Where is power concentrating, and what new capacities or possibilities are emerging?

**Democratic relations:** Who participates, who can disagree, how are different groups relating, and is harmony concealing inequality?

**Ideology and doxa:** What does the organisation reward, normalise or make seem impossible, including assumptions nobody has thought to question?

**Genealogy and unlearning:** Where did our categories and routines come from, and are we confusing professionalisation, scale or inherited custom with necessity or progress?

**Participation and access:** Who cannot use ordinary processes, what barriers produce that exclusion, and what changes are required?

These reviews should lead to decisions, assigned responsibilities and, where appropriate, collective action beyond the association rather than become symbolic reflection exercises.

### 5. Glossary of Important Terms {#part-ii-5}

This is a proposed initial glossary. It should expand or contract according to the public framework and constitution generator.

**Accountability** — The duty to explain decisions, answer to those affected, accept scrutiny and correct failures where required.

**Association** — People who voluntarily organise around a shared purpose and agree how to govern their collective activity.

**Collective efficacy** — A group's experienced belief, grounded in practice, that it can act together and affect its conditions.

**Collaboration** — People combining different knowledge, capacities and resources in shared action while retaining the ability to disagree, contest power and challenge the conditions within which they are working together.

**Commons** — Resources, knowledge, infrastructure or capacities held or governed for shared purposes under rules made or accepted by the people concerned.

**Complementarity** — A relationship in which differences contribute to shared capacity without being ranked into superior and inferior positions.

**Constitution** — The association's agreed governing rules concerning purpose, membership, authority, decision-making, resources, accountability, amendment and closure.

**Delegation** — Granting a person or body limited authority to act on behalf of the association without transferring the membership's ultimate authority.

**Democratic development** — Open-ended development of the conditions and collective capacities through which people can understand, govern and transform their common life, without assuming a universal path of progress.

**Dissent** — Expressed disagreement with a proposed or existing decision, interpretation or practice. In the DDC it is treated as a potential source of knowledge rather than disloyalty.

**Domination** — A durable relationship in which some people or institutions exercise unaccountable or structurally superior power over others.

**Doxa** — Assumptions and practices that appear so natural or obvious that they are usually reproduced without being consciously argued for.

**Federation** — Cooperation among autonomous associations through a shared body whose powers are explicitly delegated by its constituent associations.

**Freedom of association** — The freedom to form, join, support or leave organisations and act collectively with others, subject to constitutional rights and applicable law.

**Hierarchy** — An organised relation of superiority and subordination. Difference in role or expertise is not automatically hierarchy; the issue is how power is structured.

**Ideology in practice** — The ways institutions and routines continually communicate what exists, what is valued, what is possible and what kinds of social roles people are expected to occupy.

**Independent review** — Scrutiny conducted by people sufficiently independent of those whose decisions, finances or administration are being examined.

**Mandate** — The purpose, authority and limits given to a representative, delegate, committee or office-bearer.

**Member sovereignty** — The principle that ultimate authority over the association rests with its members rather than its board, staff, funders or external specialists.

**Mutual aid** — Reciprocal organisation through which people pool capacities or resources to meet shared needs, rather than one-directional charity from helpers to beneficiaries.

**Prefiguration** — Practising in present institutions the democratic relationships and forms of cooperation that people seek to make more widely possible.

**Praxis** — A recurring relationship between action and reflection through which people interpret conditions, act, learn from consequences and revise what they do.

**Reasonable accommodation** — Necessary and appropriate adjustments that enable a disabled person to participate equally where general accessibility measures are insufficient, without imposing a disproportionate burden.

**Redistribution** — Deliberate change in how material resources, opportunities, benefits or burdens are distributed, whether within an association or through wider social and public arrangements.

**Shared purpose** — The common aim that gives an association reason to exist and guides its use of resources.

**Supported participation** — Participation in which a person uses assistance, interpretation, communication support or another aid while retaining their own agency and political standing.

**Universal design** — Designing environments, information, processes and tools so they can be used by the widest practical range of people from the outset rather than relying only on later individual adjustments.

**Voluntary association** — An association formed through agreement among people rather than by compulsory membership or prior creation by the state.

The glossary used on the website should distinguish ordinary-language DDC meanings from technical legal definitions where the same term has a specific statutory meaning.

## Part III — Critical Challenges and Future Considerations {#part-iii}

The DDC is a normative framework, not a politically neutral technology. It favours voluntary association, equal standing, contestable authority, substantive participation, distributed knowledge and democratic control over shared institutions. Its commitment to plural institutional forms does not imply neutrality about domination. That makes criticism necessary: the framework should be tested not only against problems it already expects to find, but against arguments that challenge its own premises, priorities and institutional design.

The criticisms below are therefore not objections to be dismissed in advance. They identify questions that future research, participatory testing and constitutional design should keep open.

| **Criticism** | **Future consideration** |
|---|---|
| **The DDC presents itself as plural while advancing a substantive political conception of democracy.** Associations organised around paternal, clerical, managerial or customary authority may reject its assumptions. | State the normative commitments openly. Distinguish pluralism about institutional form from neutrality about domination. Test whether the framework can explain why particular limits on authority are justified rather than simply treating them as self-evident. |
| **Member sovereignty may undervalue expertise, continuity and efficient specialisation.** Not every concentration of knowledge or long-term office is domination. | Distinguish necessary specialisation from political substitution. Evaluate roles by accountability, explainability, succession, accessibility and the consequences of dependency rather than by rotation or dispersion alone. |
| **The framework may demand too much participation.** Democratic involvement consumes time, energy and attention, and intensive participation can burden precisely those with the least capacity to absorb additional work. | Treat delegation and temporary disengagement as legitimate democratic choices. Test for participation burdens, recognise unequal time and care resources, and design minimum necessary rather than maximum possible participation. |
| **Critical reflexivity can become its own professional culture.** Reviews of hierarchy, ideology, doxa and genealogy may privilege people comfortable with academic or political language. | Keep reviews proportionate, optional in form and translatable into ordinary language, stories and practical questions. Judge them by whether they improve action and accountability rather than by theoretical sophistication. |
| **The distinction between legitimate authority and hierarchy remains contestable.** Expertise, emergency coordination, care, apprenticeship and elected leadership all involve asymmetry. | Develop clearer criteria for when asymmetry becomes domination: duration, consent, accountability, reversibility, monopoly of information, material dependence and capacity to contest decisions. Test these criteria across very different association types. |
| **From a Marxist perspective, democratising associations may leave capitalist ownership and class power largely intact.** | Keep internal democracy analytically distinct from structural transformation. Examine ownership, labour, accumulation and distribution alongside organisational governance, and evaluate when association-building contributes to wider redistributive or political struggles. |
| **From a market-liberal perspective, the framework may prejudge markets, entrepreneurship, competition and professionalisation as suspect.** | Avoid treating any institutional form as inherently emancipatory or dominating. Evaluate markets, public provision, commons and cooperative arrangements through the same questions of power, dependency, distribution, ecological effects and democratic agency. |
| **The DDC has a theory of democratic institution-building but not yet a complete theory of how concentrated societal power is contested or exercised.** Federation alone does not answer questions about elections, litigation, industrial action, mass mobilisation or public administration. | Keep the DDC's scope explicit while studying how associations relate to movements, unions, parties, courts and public institutions. Future work should map different strategies of collective power without turning the DDC into a single political programme. |
| **An autonomist or anarchist critique could argue that the framework still grants too much legitimacy to the state and legal regulation.** | Preserve the distinction between public responsibility and state supremacy. Treat law and public institutions as historically variable sites of obligation, struggle and possible democratisation rather than as inherently democratic authorities. |
| **The framework may remain theoretically Eurocentric even while rejecting colonial developmentalism.** South African traditions can appear as examples interpreted through European and North American theory. | Increase the role of South African and African intellectual, movement and vernacular traditions as sources of concepts, not only cases. Participatory research should allow the framework's categories themselves to be renamed, rejected or replaced. |
| **Its treatment of inherited tradition may be asymmetrically suspicious.** Constant demands for reflexive justification can undermine forms of belonging whose value partly lies in continuity, trust and inherited obligation. | Distinguish uncritical deference from legitimate continuity. Ask not whether a practice is traditional, but whether members can understand its effects, contest serious harms and participate in its interpretation and evolution. |
| **Voluntary membership and exit can be partly fictional where belonging is economically, socially or culturally indispensable.** | Treat voluntariness as a matter of degree and material conditions rather than a binary. Constitution design should consider dependency, the consequences of exit, and protections for members who cannot realistically leave without serious loss. |
| **Member democracy can legitimate majority domination.** A majority may endorse discriminatory, punitive or exclusionary rules. | Clarify the relationship between member sovereignty and non-negotiable rights floors. Identify which equality, dignity, accessibility and due-process protections cannot be overridden by ordinary majority decision. |
| **Nonmembers can bear the consequences of member decisions without having constitutional authority.** | Strengthen horizontal accountability. For organisations with significant external effects, develop procedures for affected nonmembers, neighbouring communities, workers, ecosystems and future users to be heard without erasing member self-government. |
| **The theoretical framework may be too eclectic.** Bookchin, Rancière, Bourdieu, Therborn, Ostrom, Allen, liberation psychology and social psychology do not begin from identical assumptions about power, agency or knowledge. | Treat these traditions explicitly as lenses rather than a synthetic doctrine. Identify points of tension as well as convergence, and retain only concepts that prove useful in practice without requiring theoretical uniformity. |
| **The framework may be difficult to falsify.** If every undesirable outcome can be redescribed as hierarchy, doxa or insufficient participation, the DDC risks becoming self-confirming. | Predefine empirical questions that could challenge its expectations. Test cases where stable hierarchy, professionalisation, delegation or market mechanisms appear to improve agency, accessibility or outcomes, and revise the framework when evidence warrants it. |
| **The constitution generator can encode political judgments while appearing neutral.** Defaults, warnings and available options will shape users' choices. | Make the generator's normative assumptions visible, explain why prompts appear, offer legitimate alternatives, allow users to override recommendations where lawful, and subject interface choices to participatory review. |
| **Anti-bureaucratic safeguards can themselves produce bureaucracy.** Small associations may respond to demanding procedures by becoming more dependent on lawyers, administrators or technically confident members. | Apply proportionality as a design rule. Use the minimum governance machinery needed for the association's scale and risks, provide simple patterns before complex ones, and evaluate whether the DDC reduces or increases dependence on specialists. |

Several tensions cut across these criticisms and should remain visible as the framework develops.

**Pluralism and normativity:** the DDC permits many institutional answers but still advances substantive commitments about equality, domination and accountability. It should state those commitments plainly and remain open to argument about their boundaries.

**Democracy and organisational capacity:** participation, distributed knowledge and contestable authority can strengthen democracy, but excessive proceduralisation can weaken continuity, speed and practical effectiveness. The question is not how to maximise participation in every decision, but how to keep consequential power accountable without making collective life unworkable.

**Prefiguration and structural transformation:** democratic associations can make alternative relations real in everyday life, but prefiguration does not by itself redistribute land, wealth or institutional power. The DDC should therefore ask how internal democratic practice connects to wider collective action without prescribing one strategy of social change.

**Decoloniality and conceptual authority:** the framework cannot reject developmental hierarchy while allowing external theory to monopolise the concepts through which South African experience becomes intelligible. Its categories must remain open to reconstruction from the traditions and organisations with which it works.

These criticisms should inform the research agenda, participatory testing and generator design. A mature DDC should be capable not only of criticising the institutions that use it, but of producing evidence and arguments that could require the DDC itself to change.
